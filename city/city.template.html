Shared Sky · drone traffic simulation

With Amazon at its best-sourced heights, sharing out heights cuts planned conflicts between drone companies by 72%.

Where Amazon flies decides it. Amazon told Richardson council it flies out at about 69 m (225 ft); its FAA filings for Florida, Detroit and Kansas City put the return trip anywhere in 85–115 m, which includes Zipline’s 101 m cruise. Only if Amazon came home near the 400 ft (122 m) maximum in its filings, a height above every FAA-filed return band (at most 377 ft) that only an informal Amazon staff remark supports, would the benefit fall to about 29%. The 72% (95% interval 67–76% across 10 random layouts of Wing and Zipline sites; it does not cover model error) assumes 10 m of height between companies is enough; if 15 m were needed it would be 55%. The headline uses the filed Houston site counts, with Amazon’s real sites and Wing and Zipline sites placed at random; the animation below is a denser test patch: sites every 3 km, about 7 times as tightly packed as the filed layout, so it shows roughly 40 times more planned conflicts per square km than the headline study (120 in 2 hours over 144 km² against 45.8 an hour over 4,624 km²).

A model of a possible future build-out, not today’s traffic. With only the sites flying in Houston today, the companies’ plans almost never meet (HOUTODAY: 1–2 planned conflicts in 60 simulated hours); the question is what happens as they build out. Five of Amazon’s six Houston sites are real, geocoded locations (one, STX5, only to street level, up to about 1 km off); Wing’s and Zipline’s are placed at random because their full site lists are not public. The model flies planned paths only; it does not simulate collisions or avoidance. A “planned conflict” means two planned paths of different companies within 30 m sideways and less than 10 m vertically, with no coordination between companies. Some operators already share flight plans before take-off (the industry standard is ASTM F3548; Wing and Flytrex report doing so in DFW), so this is a reference point for how much height sharing removes, not a picture of today’s practice. The shared-out layers are 10–20 m apart, so the cut assumes 10 m between companies is enough (see Results for 15 m and 20 m).
