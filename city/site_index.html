What this is

Wing, Zipline and Amazon have each filed plans with the FAA to fly delivery drones over the same Texas cities. This simulation flies those plans together and tests one simple rule: each company keeps to its own height inside the band it already filed.

With plan sharing. Operators already share flight plans before take-off and hold a flight when its plan overlaps another company’s (a standard called ASTM F3548 covers this; I have not read it). In a modelled version of that check, flown in real 2025 wind, the check removes 99–99.6% of planned conflicts with or without the height rule. What the height rule changes is the cost: on the earlier engine about half as many flights have to wait (19% instead of 39% in Houston, 18% instead of 37% in DFW, about 6 minutes each, 10 windy hours per metro). The catch: two of the shared-out heights sit exactly 20 m apart, at the edge of what the modelled check sees, so the gaps need a margin.

Without plan sharing. As a reference point for how far plans overlap on their own, the model also flies the plans with no coordination and counts planned conflicts: moments when two companies’ planned paths come within 30 m sideways and less than 10 m in height. The height rule cuts them by about 72% (Houston) and 73% (DFW) on the earlier engine, and 61% and 72% with realistic flights.

A better set of heights. Still inside every company’s filed band, Wing at 46–52 m, Amazon at 76 m both ways and Zipline at 101 m leave at least 24 m between companies. Then only about 4% of flights wait, Amazon’s waits fall from about 82% to 18–26%, and the leftover collision risk is lower than with the shared-out heights. But that 4% needs very thin shared plans. With plans sized by the standard’s own rule (each drone must stay inside its plan 95% of the time), today’s heights hold about half of all flights and every height-sharing option about a fifth, so the headline gets stronger. The options then differ in leftover collision risk: the wider-gap heights 6–13 times lower than the shared-out heights, and letting the service move flights between heights 3–7 times higher. Better GPS helps most of all. What a narrower band costs each company is not measured. Details

How sure is this? The waiting shares come from a modelled check in windy hours (daytime hours between the 85th and 95th percentile of wind, where plans drift most); calm hours would hold fewer flights, and no operator’s real system was modelled. The halving also depends on how big each flight’s shared plan is: it holds, and grows (about half to a fifth), with plans sized by the standard’s 95% rule, but with much larger plans (±25 m in height) the shared-out heights barely help. On real Walmart site layouts the halving holds (34% → 16% Houston, 28% → 15% DFW). Most of Amazon’s flights are held either way (about 71–84%), because its climbs and descents cross every other company’s height; the halving comes from Wing and Zipline. For the cut without plan sharing, two things matter most among those tested. Amazon’s return height is not public: if it flew home near the 400 ft (122 m) maximum its filings state (a secondhand staff remark), the cut would be about 29% (Houston) or 35% (DFW). And the shared-out heights are only 10–20 m apart: across separation rules from 5 to 20 m the cut stays between 45% and 84%, but at 15 m it is about 55% (Houston) or 45% (DFW), because Amazon’s return and Zipline’s cruise sit 14 m apart. The ranges do not cover the model’s own limits: straight-line routes, uncapped overlap areas and differences between simulation engines. With only the sites flying in Houston today, the plans almost never meet; the question is the build-out.
