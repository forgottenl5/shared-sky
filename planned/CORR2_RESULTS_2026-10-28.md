## Result in one line
**With all three companies flying departure corridors, sharing out heights still cuts planned conflicts by 71 % in Houston and 82 % in DFW against best-sourced Amazon (straight routes: 72 % and 73 %).** 3 of 3 predictions passed.

## Predictions
- **C1 PASS** cut with corridors 71 % (Houston) and 82 % (DFW), inside the registered 55-85 %.
- **C2 PASS (DFW narrowly)** within 10 percentage points of the straight-route cut: Houston 71 vs 72 %; DFW 82 vs 73 %, 9 points apart, so close to the edge of the band.
- **C3 PASS** current-practice conflicts an hour with corridors are 1.09x (Houston, 37.6 vs 34.6) and 1.29x (DFW, 22.1 vs 17.1) the straight-route value, inside 0.8-1.5x.

## Reading
- Corridors raise conflicts a little where current practice already overlaps (more traffic funnelled onto shared paths) but do not change the headline: the benefit comes from separating heights, not from route shape.
- In DFW the corridors made the shared-heights arm slightly better (4.5 to 4.0 an hour) and the current-practice arm worse (17.1 to 22.1), which is why its cut is larger. With 10 seeds the difference between 73 % and 82 % is within what seed-to-seed spread could produce; do not quote DFW's corridor cut as better than its straight cut.
- Pairs: with corridors, Amazon-Zipline remains the biggest source of conflicts in both arms-of-current-practice (Houston 507, DFW 286 summed over seeds).

## Limits
- Corridors are a fitted simplification (4 per site at random bearings, 2 km out), not any company's filed routes. The only real-world check available (Richardson overflights) was reproduced with about two shared corridors, not tested at four.
- Planned conflicts only: this says nothing about actual collision risk.
